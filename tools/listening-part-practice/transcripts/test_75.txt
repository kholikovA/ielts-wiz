[69.2] Good morning, everyone.
[71.8] In the last few lectures, I've been dealing with business finance, but now I'm going to move on to business systems.
[79.7] And in today's lecture, I'm going to talk about what can go wrong when businesses try to copy their own best practices.
[88.8] Once a business has successfully introduced a new process, managing a branch bank, say, or selling a new product, the parent organisation naturally wants to repeat that success and capture it, if possible, on a bigger scale.
[106.5] The goal, then, is to utilise existing knowledge and not to generate new knowledge.
[114.1] It's a less glamorous activity than pure innovation, but it actually [[31|happens more often]], as a matter of fact.
[122.9] However, surprisingly, getting things right the second time is not necessarily any simpler than it was the first time.
[131.8] Now, there's been a lot of research into how companies can repeat their previous successes, and it certainly hasn't been confined to the United States.
[142.2] It seems that most large industries are trying to repeat their own successes and manage the knowledge they've acquired.
[152.0] But even so, it has been shown that the overwhelming majority of attempts fail.

[159.2] [[32|A host of studies confirm this]], covering a wide range of business settings: branch banks, retail stores, real estate agencies, factories, call centres, to name but a few.
[173.6] So why do so few managers get things right the second or third time?
[180.2] Let's consider one reason for failure.
[183.7] Placing too much trust in the people who are running the successful operation, the experts, shall we say.
[191.7] Managers who want to apply existing knowledge typically start off by going to an expert, such as the person who designed and is running a successful department store, and picking their brains.
[204.7] Now, [[33|this approach can be used if you want to gain a rough understanding of a particular system]] or understand smaller, isolated problems.
[215.7] The trouble is, even the expert doesn't fully grasp the whole thing, because when it comes to complex systems, the individual components of the process are interwoven with one another.
[229.0] The expert never has complete access to the necessary information.
[234.2] And the situation's complicated even further by the fact that experts are usually not aware of their own ignorance.
[244.1] The ignorance can take various forms.
[247.8] For instance, a lot of details of the system are invisible to managers.
[253.9] [[34|Some may be difficult to describe]], learned on the job and well-known by workers, perhaps, but impossible to describe in a way that's helpful.

[264.6] And there are some things that people know or do that they're not even aware of.

[275.4] Now, let's consider two types of mistake that can occur when a manager actually starts to set up a duplicate system to replicate a successful process.
[287.4] Firstly, perhaps he forgets that he was just trying to copy another process and starts trying to improve on it.
[297.8] Another mistake is trying to use the best parts of various different systems in the hope of creating the perfect [[35|combination]].

[308.2] Unfortunately, attempts like these usually turn out to be misguided and lead to problems.
[315.8] Why?
[317.1] Well, for various reasons.
[320.2] Perhaps there weren't really any advantages after all, because the information wasn't accurate.
[326.9] Or perhaps the business settings weren't really comparable.

[332.5] More typically, the advantages are real enough, but there are also disadvantages that have been overlooked.
[341.7] For example, the modifications might compromise [[36|safety]] in some way.
[348.7] So what's the solution?
[351.2] Well, I don't intend to suggest that it's easy to get things right the second time.
[357.7] It's not.
[359.2] But the underlying problem has more to do with attitudes than the actual difficulty of the task, and there are ways of getting it right.
[370.4] These involve adjusting [[37|attitudes]], first of all.
[375.3] Being more realistic and cautious, really.
[380.0] Secondly, they involve exerting strict [[38|controls]] on the organisational and operational systems.

[388.8] And this, in turn, means copying the original as closely as possible.
[394.9] Not merely duplicating the physical characteristics of the [[39|factory]], but also duplicating the [[40|skills]] that the original employees had.
[406.4] Reliance on a template like this offers the huge advantage of built-in consistency.
