[70.2] Good morning.
[72.1] Today, we're thinking about the way that technology is influencing our social structures and the way we interact with one another.
[80.7] Humans, as we know, have always lived in groups.
[85.4] Without this arrangement, our species would have died out long ago.
[90.5] But now, the way we see and define our group is changing.
[96.5] I'd like to start by mentioning the research of American sociologist Mark Granovetter in 1973.
[104.7] It was Granovetter who first coined the term "weak ties", which he used to refer to people's loose acquaintances.
[114.6] In other words, friends of friends.
[117.8] His research showed that weak ties had a significant effect on the behaviour and choices of populations, and this influence was something highly important in the fields of information science and politics, and as you can imagine, [[31|marketing]] also.

[136.9] So, these friends of friends, people we might spend time with at social or work gatherings, might not be like us, but they can still have a positive influence because we share the same sort of [[32|interests]].
[154.0] That's enough to make a connection, and this connection can turn out to be more beneficial than we might suspect.
[161.7] An example of this, an example of how the connection can influence us, is when our weak ties get in touch and pass on details about [[33|jobs]] they think might be suitable for us.
[175.2] Well, since Granovetter first came up with this theory, his work has been cited in over 19,000 papers.
[184.0] Some of these studies have looked at how weak tie networks are useful to us in other ways, and one thing that seems to improve as a result of weak tie influence is our [[34|health]].

[201.2] Today, our number of weak-tie acquaintances has exploded due to the Internet, to the phenomenon of online social networking.
[211.7] This is still a relatively new way of communication, something that has a huge amount of potential.
[218.8] But also, as with any invention, it brings with it a new set of problems.

[226.9] Let's start with the benefits.
[230.0] Without question, online social networking allows us to pass on the latest news, to be [[35|up-to-date with local and global events, and for many, this information comes from sources more trustworthy]] than local media.
[247.2] So this is one clear point in favour of online social networking.
[253.0] I know that it's also being used by students, as a means of increasing their chances of success in the way that lecture notes can be shared and ideas discussed.
[266.2] I think, personally speaking, that we need some further research before we can definitively say whether it helps or not.
[275.8] There's also been a great increase in the number of networking sites devoted to sharing advice on health issues, but there are as yet no studies to prove the reliability of that advice.
[289.9] Now, what we do have clear evidence for is that people are [[36|developing friendships and professional networks in a way that wasn't possible before]].
[301.2] The process is faster.
[303.9] I'm not talking about quality here, but simply that they exist.

[309.0] And it's debatable whether the number of online friends that you have increases your level of self-confidence.
[317.1] That's perhaps an area of research some of you might be interested in following up.

[324.0] Turning to the problems, there are any number of articles connecting online activity to falling levels of physical fitness.
[332.9] But it's too easy to blame the Internet for our social problems.
[337.9] The poor grades of schoolchildren are also frequently linked to the time spent on social networking sites, but it would be naive to believe there are no other contributing factors.

[351.2] One real concern, however, is the [[37|increase in the amount of fraud]], where, for example, people are using the personal data of others which they've put online for criminal purposes.
