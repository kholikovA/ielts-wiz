[71.1] In today's lecture, I'm going to talk about monosodium glutamate, or MSG as it's more commonly known.
[80.0] Now, MSG, as you probably know, is a flavour enhancer, which is used particularly in Chinese and Japanese cooking.
[88.5] Today, I am going to explore why it is so popular in these cuisines, and more importantly, [[31|how does it enhance the flavour of food]]?
[100.5] The main reason why MSG is more commonly used in Japanese meals is tradition.
[107.3] For many thousands of years, the Japanese have incorporated a type of seaweed known as kombu in their cooking, as they discovered it had the ability to make food taste better.
[119.7] But it wasn't until 1908 that the ingredient in kombu, which was responsible for the improvement in flavour, was actually [[32|discovered to be glutamate by scientists working there]].
[133.1] From 1908 until 1956, glutamate was produced commercially in Japan by a very slow and expensive means of extraction.
[143.3] It was in 1956 that the speed of the process was improved and [[33|industrial production increased dramatically]] and still continues to increase to this day.
[155.4] In fact, hundreds of thousands of tons of MSG are produced all over the world today.

[166.4] So what exactly is MSG?
[169.2] Well, monosodium glutamate contains 78.2% glutamate, 12.2% sodium, and 9.6% [[34|water]].
[182.2] Glutamate is an amino acid that can be found naturally in all protein-containing foods.
[188.7] So this includes food such as [[35|meat]] and [[36|cheese]].
[193.7] It is widely known that Chinese and Japanese food contains MSG.
[199.2] But many people don't seem to be aware that it is also used in foods in other parts of the world.
[204.9] For example, it is found in commercially made Italian pizzas, in American fast food, and in Britain, MSG is used in things like potato crisps.
[216.7] So, how exactly does MSG work?
[220.5] Well, in the Western world, we commonly talk of four tastes, and I'm sure you're all familiar with the concepts of sweet, sour, bitter, and salt.

[232.5] Well, in 1908, Kikunae Ikeda identified a [[37|fifth taste]], and it is thought that MSG intensifies this naturally occurring taste in some food.
[245.2] It does make perfect evolutionary sense that we should have the ability to detect or taste glutamate because it is the amino acid which is most [[38|common]] in natural foods.

[258.5] John Prescott, an associate professor at the University of Chicago, suggests that this fifth taste serves a purpose just as the other tastes do.
[268.8] He suggests that it signals to us the presence of protein in food in the same way that sweetness indicates that a food contains energy-giving carbohydrates.
[280.5] [[39|Bitterness]], he says, alerts us to toxins in the food, while sourness warns us of spoilage, and saltiness signals the presence of [[40|minerals]].
